# Decision Filter

## What This Guide Is For

Founders make dozens of decisions a week. Most are fine. A few are load-bearing — they compound in one direction or the other for years. This filter helps identify which is which, and get clear on the right call.

---

## The Five-Question Filter

Run these in order. Stop when you have clarity.

**1. Is this actually a decision — or am I avoiding the discomfort of committing?**
Many "decisions" are already made. The client knows what they want to do; they're looking for permission or reassurance. If the answer would change with more information, it's a real decision. If more information wouldn't change anything, it's avoidance.

**2. What is the cost of not deciding?**
Indecision is itself a choice. Name what staying stuck is costing: time, momentum, team confidence, opportunity cost. This often clarifies urgency.

**3. Which option is reversible — and which isn't?**
For reversible decisions: decide quickly, learn fast. For irreversible decisions: slow down, get more data. Most founder decisions are more reversible than they feel in the moment.

**4. What would I advise a founder I respect to do in this situation?**
Psychological distance clarifies. The answer that comes back is usually the right one — the hard part is applying it to yourself.

**5. Which option do I already know I'm going to choose?**
Ask directly. Most clients already know. The question is whether they'll own it.

---

## When Fear Is the Real Block

If the client is circling a decision they know they should make but won't, it's rarely about the decision. It's about what the decision means:
- Committing to one path means closing others (loss aversion)
- Making the call means being accountable for the outcome (fear of failure)
- Moving forward means leaving current safety (comfort zone attachment)

Name what's actually happening. The decision is rarely the problem.

---

## Coaching Notes

- Don't help them make the decision — help them make it themselves. The goal is decision confidence, not dependency.
- Second-guessing a made decision is almost never productive. If no new material information has surfaced, close it.
- Watch for "analysis paralysis" — the compulsive need for more data before committing. It's rarely about data.

---

## Questions to Use in Session

- What would you decide if you couldn't get this wrong?
- What's the worst realistic outcome of each option — and can you live with it?
- If you had to decide in the next 10 minutes, what would you choose?
- What would make you regret this decision in 5 years?
- Is there information that would actually change your answer — or are you looking for permission?
